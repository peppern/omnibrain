## Main Point

Doris Tsao's lab reports a monkey study (bioRxiv preprint led by Janis Hesse) finding that face-processing cortex flips about 15 times a second between representing what the animal consciously sees and what is suppressed from awareness, so consciousness looks like a matter of timing rather than of a particular brain region.

## Takeaways

- Method: Neuropixels recordings from thousands of neurons across face patches in inferotemporal and prefrontal cortex during binocular flash suppression, where one image is consciously seen while a second, still physically present, is kept out of awareness.
- Activity alternates between two coordinated micro-states roughly every 60 ms; decoding one state reconstructs the conscious face and decoding the other reconstructs the suppressed face in rich detail.
- The same neurons switch their tuning between the conscious and the suppressed stimulus; the two states can still be told apart, with feature tuning amplified during conscious micro-epochs.
- This explains a long-standing puzzle: averaged over time, conscious modulation looks weak (contrary to the textbook claim that high-level visual neurons track only the perceived stimulus), because the averaged signal mixes the two opposite states.
- Tsao argues that finding a clear signal for the unseen stimulus deep in the brain makes it tractable to study how a representation becomes conscious; the abstract concludes conscious access needs temporal selection, not just localization. This is a preprint and the claims are the authors' own.
